Wilhelm Abegg

Philipp Friedrich Wilhelm Abegg · Berlin 1876 – Baden-Baden 1951

Origins and training

Philipp Friedrich Wilhelm Abegg was born in Berlin on 29 August 1876. He attended the Wilhelms-Gymnasium there, studied law, took his doctorate in 1903 and passed the Great State Examination (große Staatsprüfung) in 1904.

Provincial administration, 1904–1920

As a Regierungsassessor he served at Waldenburg in Silesia until July 1906, when he was transferred to the regional government in Oppeln. In August 1906 he was appointed deputy chairman of the workers' insurance tribunal (Schiedsgericht für Arbeiterversicherung) for the Oppeln district. A further appointment to the post was announced in 1909 and revoked in May 1910. In November 1912, by then a Regierungsrat, he was moved from Oppeln to the Royal Police Headquarters (Polizeipräsidium) in Berlin.

Police reformer and State Secretary

Called into the Prussian Interior Ministry in August 1920, he headed its police division and directed the reorganisation of the Prussian police. On his fiftieth birthday in August 1926 the Berliner Tageblatt called him the "creator of the modern Prussian police". In early 1927 the government told the Prussian Landtag that the reorganisation was essentially complete, that the main credit belonged to Abegg, and that he was owed the thanks of the state government. In October 1926 he was appointed State Secretary in the Prussian Interior Ministry. He was a member of the German Democratic Party (DDP).

A public republican

On 18 May 1928 he spoke on "Kampf um die Republik" to the Young Democrats (Jungdemokratische Bewegung) at the Lehrervereinshaus on Alexanderplatz, alongside the youth leader Normann Körber. That spring he published Die preußische Verwaltung und ihre Reform – Länder und Reich (Sieben-Stäbe-Verlag), which the press cited in the debate on Prussian administrative reform. In October 1928 he was elected president of the Reich Association of Mixed Choirs of Germany (Reichsverband der gemischten Chöre Deutschlands). He still held the office in April 1930, when the association's annual meeting closed with a concert of about a thousand singers from sixteen Berlin choirs. In June 1929 he spoke publicly on the tasks of women in police work.

Conflicts, 1930–1932

In November 1930 a Berlin court acquitted him in a private defamation suit brought by Senatspräsident Grützner. At Interior Minister Grzesinski's farewell in March 1930, Abegg had spoken of "slanderers and base fellows", and Grützner, whose letter to Minister-President Braun had preceded the resignation, took this as aimed at him. The court found that Abegg had acted to protect legitimate interests. On 4 June 1932 he met the Communist deputies Ernst Torgler and Wilhelm Kasper. After the Prussian coup the Papen government presented the meeting as evidence of collusion with the KPD. In early November 1932 the Papen administration released Rudolf Diels's protocol of the meeting to the press. Abegg's lawyer Ernst Feder answered with Abegg's own account: he had urged the Communists to stop acts of terror, and Diels himself had proposed the meeting.

Dismissal and exile

With the Preußenschlag of 20 July 1932 he was removed from office. On 1 August 1932 the Reichsanzeiger announced that he had been placed in provisional retirement (einstweiliger Ruhestand). At the end of February 1933 he fled to Switzerland and worked in Zürich as a lawyer in international law until 1949. He had been seeking to regain Swiss citizenship from Berlin since 1930. On 29 April 1941 the Reichsanzeiger published a notice, dated 24 April, depriving him and his wife Hildegard, née Hofmeier, of German citizenship. After a stroke in 1948 he died in Baden-Baden on 18 October 1951.
